# War Idol — style guide

Working document. Everything here was decided somewhere in the writing and is
easy to lose between sessions. Nothing is sacred; if a rule is wrong, change it
here first so it stops applying everywhere.

---

## 1. Tense

**Scenes are present. Summarised past events stay past.**

- Present: the recruiter, the fall, the bar, the alley. *"I about-face." · "She snatches it away." · "I accept it."*
- Past, deliberately: the discharge, the buddy's death, the boom and the fathers, the infestation exposition, the bar-crawl and the fight. Those *are* past, and keeping them past makes the shifts meaningful rather than accidental.

**Don't mix tense inside a single predication** — one subject, one verb chain. Two verbs, one action, two tenses: that's the shape of the error. The fall on the stairs used to read *"…and then my cheek **dug** hard into the red pavement"* — the file now says **digs**, because the shift wasn't doing any work.

**But some shifts inside one sentence are standard, and stay.** A past event with a present-tense consequence, or a past report with a present-tense truth:

- *"when the Ministry sanctioned individuals to cover the plight on Io, it gives them authority"* — the act is past, the effect is now.
- *"They called it many things as if they knew what happened, but the truth is no one knows"* — the report is past, the truth is present.

Those are not slips, and "fixing" them would be the mistake. The test is whether the two verbs are the *same* action. If they are, one tense. If the second one is a *consequence* or a *judgement made now*, the shift is doing work.

## 2. The narrator

Hardboiled, dry, terse. He reports; he does not reflect.

**Two axes, and they are independent: *flat* is the delivery, *specific* is the content.** Do not collapse them.

- The **tone** never rises — no ornament, no drama, no lyric register, no word the man would be proud of.
- The **nouns** never go generic — *gore* over *blood*, *knife-rent* over *mutilated*, *the Horned* over *the enemy*, the unusual word order over the expected one.

**So "plainer" is not the same as "flatter."** Reaching for a safer synonym makes the prose **less flat and less specific at once**, because a plainer noun is a more *worn* noun, and worn nouns are the generic ones. When the baseline register is this dry, the variance has to come from somewhere — and it comes from word choice and word order, never from raising the temperature.

**And variance is spent deliberately.** The flat baseline is what *buys* the exception: a beautiful or technical word inside a beautiful paragraph is worth nothing, while the same word in a sea of nouns is an event. Budget it — **one or two bursts a scene, never two in a paragraph, and never the same burst twice.** This is what licenses a line like *The Louche Effect.*: a term dropped into plain prose reads as a burst, where the same term inside a paragraph of explanation would read as the author enjoying their own research.

**He keeps the subject when he acts, and drops it when he's acted upon.**

| he acts | he is acted upon |
|---|---|
| *"I pull out my las-pistol"* | *"Three recoils."* |
| *"I grab her wrist"* | *"A pipe across my back."* |
| *"I hobble to her"* | *"Four of those spent suspended on a torture rack"* |

That's not a fragment tic — it's dissociation, and the grammar does it for you. The torture is the one place in the chapter where he is purely the patient, so the "I" is correctly absent.

**He amends names privately.** *"Ex-Nemean, I think to myself."* · *"and I ain't an old man..."* He corrects labels in his head and out the side of his mouth, never to anyone's face. Two instances of this make it a habit — use it.

**He does not editorialise about her.** The same man who calls her *the goblin* and *the pink pixie* does not say *divine nymph*. Where she is elevated (Daphne, alabaster) the elevation is *earned* by something that just happened, and it is reported flat.

## 3. Naming and register

**One word per tribe. The noun identifies the speaker; you never have to write "she works in media."**

| register | term |
|---|---|
| paperwork / propaganda | **the Revelation** |
| what the Feed sells | **the Apocalypse** |
| what people say | **the Horned**, bugs, bots |
| the briefing / the form | **Sectoid** — what it *resembles*, not what it is |
| the Feed | **the Kiss** |
| workers | **the pass** |
| marines | **a dry hump** |
| the platform | **the Feed** · derisively **the Trough** · **the Tube/Tuber** |
| the credential | **the tick** — ticketed, unticked |
| the stage on the vehicle | **the ring** |

Articles carry meaning too, and they are **lowercase, part of the name**: *the pass* is the institution, *a dry hump* is one instance, *the Kiss* is a brand, *a White Cow* is a drink. So it is **the Revelation** mid-sentence, never *The Revelation*.

**And the capital is reserved.** A register term takes a lowercase article in everyone's *mouth* — narration, dialogue, the chatter of the Feed. The capital belongs to the **Ministry's own surfaces**: a poster, a sign, a form, a licence, a broadcast ident, a letterhead. *The Revelation* in sender-sans is the branding; *the Revelation* in a sentence is the street repeating it. That gap is the spine rendered as typography — the same logic as *the pass* against *a dry hump* — so spend it deliberately rather than by habit.

**The enemy has three words, and they are not synonyms — they are places to stand.**

| word | who says it | what it is |
|---|---|---|
| **bugs** · **bots** | the grunts | affection or contempt, and nothing else |
| **the Horned** | the civilians | what it *looks like* from across the street |
| **Sectoid** | the briefing | a taxonomy word, clipped from *insectoid* — used by anyone who wants to sound briefed |

*A fourth word for the same thing would be a synonym, and synonyms are the enemy of this system. Keep the three separate.* *Sectoid* is Chip's because official vocabulary is free prestige and she takes what is free — the same instinct as everything else she does.

**And *bugs* is the word the lie is built on, so the narrator keeps it.** Vermin get *exterminated*; beasts get *fought*. The Ministry's method is contempt rather than fear — which is why the visor weaponises disgust — and *bugs* is what makes the enemy pest control instead of a war. So his contempt is load-bearing, and it is **supposed to be wrong**.

*Beasts* is not a synonym and is not banned. It is his **reflective** word, where *bugs* is his **reactive** one:

| when | word |
|---|---|
| he is angry, tired, bleeding | **bugs** |
| he is summing up, being considered | **beasts** |
| a civilian is talking | **the Horned** |
| someone is writing it down | **Sectoid** |

In the prologue the slur sits three lines before *"Wait, that's not right. You're not…"*, and that doubt only works because the word he has just used was cheap. **Do not let *beasts* become his default**, or he ends up standing above the crowd he belongs to.

**And the form itself never names the creature.** A Ministry paper files the *condition*: an incident, a consolidation, a write-off. The desk names the loss, not the monster. That is the useful gap — the paperwork has an entry for what happened to a man and no entry for what he became.

*The name is often the lie* (§5) applies hardest here: *Sectoid* is accurate about the resemblance and silent about the person.

## 4. Reference and allusion

**He names things. He does not gesture at them.**

His myth references are all proper nouns — **Io, Juno, Argus Panoptes, Cicero, Nemean, Vulkan**. *"The bearing of a divine nymph"* is what someone says when they don't know which nymph; *"Bernini's Daphne"* is what he says. Specificity is the tell, and it's the character.

**And his common nouns skew epic too.** *Gore*, not *blood*. It comes from Old English *gor* — filth, dung — and it is the word English epic translation reaches for: the field after a slaughter, swords drunk with it. A man who reads the world through myth does not say *blood*; that's a liquid. **Gore is the aftermath as a material.** The modern horror-fandom sense (*gore film*, *gorehound*) is a reader's reflex, not a property of the word, so do not let it scare you off the epic noun. It is his.

**And the one thing he can't name is load-bearing.** *"...something older underneath it that I haven't got a name for."* A man who names everything failing to name something is the strongest move available. Don't spend it.

**Never explain an allusion.** Cicero's tongue, Daphne's laurel — readers who know get it, readers who don't lose nothing. Explaining kills it, and the joke is never worth more than the allusion.

**Myth pays off in plot, not in prose.** Naming Cicero and then having her *talk him into a contract* is the payoff. Naming Cicero and then mentioning how he died is a footnote.

## 5. Euphemism is the house style

The world names catastrophes like a press office: **the Giant Leap Pioneer Project. The White Cow. The Kiss. The Revelation.** Never name a disaster plainly. That's how real institutions behave and it's the whole satire — the paperwork survives, the meaning changes.

Corollary: **the name is often the lie.** *The Revelation* claims the truth came out. It didn't.

---

## 6. Puns belong to characters, not to the narration

**Characters make jokes at each other. The narration notices consequences.**

*"Old-fashioned huh? But I don't mind that."* is a character being clever. If the *prose* starts winking, the narrator becomes a man performing that he's been through it, and the sincerity built on the stairs and the red pavement is spent.

---

## 7. The Eye

Jupiter's Great Red Spot is the indifferent watcher — *"that mocking red eye."* The visor and the Feed are the **manufactured** eye, and the licence is a **glass badge of an eye**. Keep them rhyming.

The Eye can be **blotted out** — cloud kills Jupiter. The rain is the only privacy on Io. Anything that hides the Eye is a gift; use it for a man who is otherwise always being filmed.

---

## 8. Props do the characterisation

- **His old-fashioned** vs **her martini glass** — tradition and deliberation against glamour and speed. A rocks glass and a cone on the same bar.
- **His tantalum cane** — the implant metal, and the thing they'd put *inside* him. He refuses the graft and leans on it.
- **The cloak** — the emblem rides on the *garment*, not the body, which is what lets him take it off and give it to her. The lion changes owners, and nobody says so.
- **The drinks** — four glasses is a stunt. That's her.

---

## 9. What the narrator never does

- Explains an allusion.
- Compliments her.
- Says a brand's word for something when a worker's word exists.
- Uses a pun the *narration* is proud of.

## 10. Engine syntax

```
** bold **      * italic *      _ italic _      ,, sub ,,      ^^ sup ^^
```
Subscript and superscript are custom (`car/vn/script_effect.gd`) — stock Godot BBCode has neither. Single `^x^` also works for superscript. Tilde is **not** a mark; Chip's lines use `~` as style and it renders literally.

**The full set, in the order the engine parses it:**

| form | result |
|---|---|
| `***x***` | **bold italic** |
| `___x___` | **bold italic** |
| `**x**` | **bold** |
| `__x__` | **bold** |
| `*x*` | *italic* |
| `_x_` | *italic* |
| `^^x^^` / `^x^` | superscript |
| `,,x,,` | subscript |
| backticks | `[code]`, monospace |
| `\*` `\_` `\`` `\^` `\,` | a **literal** mark, no formatting |

Longer marks must run first — `***` before `**` before `*` — or the shorter pass eats the longer one. That is why `__` and `___` are listed even though `_` alone is italic: `__bold__` with only an `_` pass produces garbage. The escapes are parked in control characters for the duration of the passes and restored afterwards, which is what stops a stray mark from mangling a line.

**Not marks here:** `~~strike~~`, `==highlight==`, `[text](url)`, `<b>`. The first two would collide with Chip's tildes and with the label syntax (`== beat`); links and HTML have no use in a VN. For strikethrough, underline, code blocks or links, write BBCode directly — `[s]`, `[u]`, `[codeblock]`, `[url=…]` — the engine passes it through untouched.

**One hazard left, from how the wrapper works.** It splits on the mark and wraps every *other* segment, so **two or more unescaped marks in a line** can close and open spans you never intended — one underscore is safe (`leg_pool` is untouched), two are not, and the fix is to escape the one you meant literally. And the pass order above is load-bearing; don't reorder it.

**Directives**

```
@ img <name>   @ sfx <name>   @ music <name>   @ cast A=Alpha, B
@ page         @ zoom <f>     @ pan <dx> <dy> [sec]   @ sat <v> [sec]
@ fade [s]     @ glitch [s]   @ shake          @ stage <text>
@ input_name   @ fast [mult]  @ fast off       @ preset name = fx + fx
```

- **A line is dialogue only if the text before the first `:` is ONE word, or matches a name on `@ cast`.** So `Chip:` works; `Our contract: 50/50` stays narration; `Captain Keno:` needs the cast line.
- **`---` alone on a line means `@ page`.** Both still work; `---` is the readable form and is preferred in new prose. Three or more hyphens, nothing else on the line.
- **A mistyped directive fails silently.** No error, no effect. Check anything starting with `@` against the list above.
- **One line = one click.** Line breaks are pacing. `|` inside a line is an extra click mid-line.
- **Switching between narration and dialogue clears the accumulated narration page.** Use `@ page` at scene breaks and `@ stage` for short interior beats that sit between dialogue.
- **`@ fast` is sticky** (a mode). `@ fast` alone is 4×, `@ fast off` resets. Covers a *run* of lines — a chat flood, radio traffic. `@ fast 100` is effectively instant.

## 11. File conventions

- Scene files live in `car/vn/scenes/`, named `NN_scene.txt`. Files starting `_` are ignored by the manifest scanner.
- **Plain-text reading copies** are generated by `powershell -File Notes\make_plain.ps1`. They strip every
  directive and comment, keep the prose, turn a page break into a bare `---`, and expand the cast
  shorthand to display names. **The break is never dropped** — a reading copy without it loses the
  scene's structure, which is the whole point of reading one.
- **Audio is split by kind**: one-shot effects in `car/vn/sfx/`, looping music in `car/vn/music/`. `@ sfx` and `@ music` resolve to their own folder, and the two have different import needs — music is streamed and looped, effects are short and played many at once.
- **Lowercase asset filenames.** `keno.png`, not `Keno.png` — case sensitivity will bite on export.
- Re-run `powershell -File Notes\commission.ps1` after writing; it regenerates `Notes/asset_commission.md`, the list of every img/sfx/music the scripts ask for and which are still missing.

## 12. Art direction

**Medium for this project: digital. Tool: Clip Studio Paint.** The oil practice sets the
*look*, not the tool.

**The register split — two voices, and they never trade places:**

| layer | register | source |
|---|---|---|
| avatars, HUD, the Feed, the licence, broadcast idents | **graphic and legible** — flat colour, bold silhouette, designed type, high contrast | the *CH4NGE* poster mode |
| the world — ground, sky, structures, interiors, the enemy | **painterly and atmospheric** — wet brush, aerial perspective, limited palette | the oil studies |

Io is a palette problem before it is a drawing problem: sulfur yellow-green, ochre, red on
eruption, yellow-white on the dusted ice. Hold one palette and one sky across every plate.

**The material test.** Does it have dirt on it, and does it have a repair? Grit comes from
wear — stitching, scuffs, dents, sulfur dust in creases, a leg four years old. It dies the
moment materials become effortless: glossy eye highlights, pastel haze, hair that never gets
dirty, rim-light on a beaten character.

**The enemy design law.** The Horned must look like a **rendering**, not a creature — UI-like
gloss, eerie regularity, over-clean surfaces. And every "insect" feature must **trace back to
a piece of gear**: the round toothed pauldrons became the horns. On a second look the viewer
should be able to find the pauldron inside the horn. This is what makes the twist pay off
retroactively on the art, and it is checkable on every enemy asset.

**Two drills, learned only when a scene needs them.** One-point for interiors; two-point for
exteriors containing a manufactured object. Drawabox for box and ellipse discipline,
Robertson for constructed space, Loomis or Reilly for constructive figure work. Do not take a
course — solve the setup the current scene demands, then stop.

**Pass order for a plate.** thumbnails at 3 cm → three values, no colour → layout → flat local
colour → light → material → atmosphere and grade → export. Never start a pass before the last
one is solved.

**The zoom rule.** Work at the whole-canvas zoom you would judge a 20x35 cm painting at. Go
closer only for cleanup. Over-zooming is what kills a traditional painter's eye on a tablet.

**Compose wider than 16:9** if the plate will ever use `@ pan` — the engine clamps panning to
the plate overhang, so a plate that is exactly 16:9 cannot pan at all.

## Pre-flight for a new scene

1. Tense: scenes present, backstory past. No mixing within a predication — but a past event with a present consequence is correct, not a slip.
2. Every `@` directive exists in the list above.
3. `@ page` at scene breaks; no mode-flip stutter.
4. Names: one word per tribe, and the noun tells you who's talking.
5. Any myth or historical reference is a proper noun, and unexplained.
6. No mechanism explained, no allusion explained, no compliment from the narrator.
7. Re-run the commission script.
